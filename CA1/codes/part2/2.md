# Part 2: Concurrency, Scheduling, and Context Switching Analysis

This section provides a detailed analysis of the Go scheduler's behavior based on our benchmark results. The experiment evaluated two types of workloads (`CPU-bound` and `Mixed`) across varying logical processors (`GOMAXPROCS`) and goroutine counts.

Below are the answers to the core analytical questions regarding the expected outcomes of this benchmark:

### 1. What happens to the execution time as the number of goroutines increases?
The effect on execution time heavily depends on the workload type and the available CPU cores:
* **CPU-Bound Workload:** If `GOMAXPROCS=1`, increasing the number of goroutines does *not* decrease the execution time. Instead, the total time increases because the single core must execute the same amount of mathematical operations while also paying the hidden penalty of context-switching between goroutines. When `GOMAXPROCS > 1`, execution time decreases significantly, but only up to the point where the number of goroutines equals the number of physical cores.
* **Mixed Workload (I/O Bound):** Increasing the number of goroutines effectively hides the latency of the blocking operations (e.g., `time.Sleep`). While the total execution time for the entire batch increases, the overlapping of wait times means the workload is completed much faster than if it were executed sequentially.

### 2. Does increasing the number of goroutines always increase throughput?
**No.** Throughput behavior diverges based on the workload:
* For **CPU-bound tasks**, throughput hits a hard ceiling. In our benchmark on a single core, throughput flatlined at around `~275 ops/sec` regardless of whether we used 1 or 64 goroutines. Over-provisioning goroutines for pure CPU tasks does not increase throughput.
* For **Mixed tasks**, increasing goroutines *does* increase throughput. Even on a single core, throughput doubled from `~45 ops/sec` to `~91 ops/sec` as we scaled up to 64 goroutines, because the Go scheduler multiplexes active computation while other goroutines are blocked.

### 3. What is the difference in behavior between `GOMAXPROCS=1` and `GOMAXPROCS=NumCPU`?
This comparison highlights the difference between **Concurrency** and **True Parallelism**:
* `GOMAXPROCS=1` forces strict concurrency. Multiple goroutines take turns executing on a single OS thread. It is highly efficient for I/O-bound tasks but offers zero speedup for mathematical calculations.
* `GOMAXPROCS=NumCPU` (e.g., 12 cores) enables true parallelism. Goroutines execute simultaneously across distinct physical cores. In our benchmark, this caused the CPU-bound throughput to violently jump from `~275 ops/sec` to `~1477 ops/sec`, drastically reducing the average latency per task.

### 4. In which type of workload are the effects of scheduling and switching more visible?
The effects of scheduling are highly visible in **Mixed (I/O-heavy) workloads**. 
When a goroutine hits a blocking operation (`time.Sleep`), it voluntarily yields the CPU. The Go scheduler must actively save its state, find another runnable goroutine, and perform a context switch. We tracked this using our custom `Total Blocks` metric and Standard Deviation (`Std Dev`). The Mixed workload exhibited high variance and latency fluctuations (Std Dev in milliseconds), proving that the scheduler was aggressively swapping tasks in and out of the run queue. Conversely, the CPU-bound tasks showed microscopic variance (Std Dev in microseconds), indicating uninterrupted execution slices (high fairness).

### 5. At what point does increasing concurrency cease to be useful or even decrease performance?
The point of diminishing returns depends entirely on hardware and the nature of the task:
* **For pure CPU-bound tasks:** Concurrency ceases to be useful exactly when **`Goroutines > NumCPU`**. Spawning 64 goroutines on a 12-core machine for math operations forces the CPU to waste cycles saving and restoring registers (Context Switching Overhead) instead of calculating. Performance begins to degrade beyond this threshold.
* **For I/O or Mixed tasks:** The threshold is much higher. You can comfortably spawn thousands of goroutines for network or disk operations. Performance will only degrade when the sheer memory footprint of the goroutines (stack allocations) or the scheduler's queue management overhead exceeds the system's capacity.

### Benchmark Results Analysis: Concurrency vs. Parallelism

The generated dataset provides a perfect empirical demonstration of core operating system concepts, specifically regarding the Go scheduler, concurrency, and true parallelism. Below is the technical breakdown of the observed behaviors:

#### 1. CPU-Bound Workloads: The Glass Ceiling of Concurrency
* **The Illusion of Speed on a Single Core (`GOMAXPROCS=1`):** As seen in the single-core CPU-bound rows, whether utilizing 1 or 64 goroutines, the throughput hits a hard ceiling (plateauing between **740 to 790 ops/sec**). This proves that time-slicing a pure mathematical workload on a single physical core yields no performance gain; the CPU merely divides its finite time among the tasks.
* **The Power of True Parallelism (`GOMAXPROCS=12`):** When the logical processor count increases to 12, true parallelism is achieved. The throughput scales dramatically from **800 ops/sec** (with 1 goroutine) to an impressive **4,271 ops/sec** (with 64 goroutines). The system effectively utilizes multiple physical cores to execute mathematical computations simultaneously.
* **Scheduler Fairness:** Across all CPU-bound tests, the Standard Deviation (`Std Dev`) remains microscopic (in the **µs** range). This highlights the remarkable fairness of the Go scheduler, ensuring that CPU time is distributed almost perfectly evenly among all active goroutines.

#### 2. Mixed Workloads: The Art of Latency Hiding
* **Throughput Scaling on a Single Core:** Unlike CPU-bound tasks, the Mixed workload exhibits massive performance gains even on a single core. Scaling from 1 to 64 goroutines increases the throughput from **59 ops/sec** to **266 ops/sec** (over a 4x improvement).
* **The Role of Blocking (Concurrency):** The `Total Blocks` column explains this phenomenon perfectly. As goroutines voluntarily sleep (`time.Sleep`), they yield the processor. Instead of leaving the CPU idle, the Go scheduler immediately swaps in another runnable goroutine from the queue. This is the textbook definition of **Concurrency**: hiding the wait times of one task by executing another.

#### 3. The Hidden Cost: Context Switching Overhead
* The benchmark beautifully exposes the penalty of context switching through the **Standard Deviation (Std Dev)** and **Max Latency** metrics.
* In Mixed workloads with a high number of goroutines (e.g., 32 or 64), the variance spikes significantly (e.g., **21.44 ms** on 1 core, **10.09 ms** on 2 cores). Waking up dozens of sleeping goroutines and managing their re-entry into the run queue is inherently unpredictable. This overhead causes extreme fluctuations in completion times, pushing the `Max Latency` as high as **152.60 ms**.

#### Conclusion
This data conclusively proves a fundamental rule of distributed systems and operating systems: **For pure computational tasks (CPU-bound), true parallelism (more physical cores) is strictly required to increase throughput. However, for tasks involving wait times or I/O (Mixed workloads), high concurrency (more goroutines) drastically improves hardware utilization and overall efficiency, even on a single core.**






### Graphical Analysis of Scheduler Performance

#### Plot 1: Scalability & Throughput (Concurrency vs. Parallelism)
* **Observation:** The line chart illustrates the throughput (ops/sec) against the number of goroutines on a logarithmic scale. For `GOMAXPROCS=1`, the `CPU-bound` workload forms a flat horizontal line (a "glass ceiling"), capping at roughly 790 ops/sec regardless of the goroutine count. In contrast, for `GOMAXPROCS=12`, the throughput for the same workload scales aggressively, peaking over 4,200 ops/sec.
* **Conclusion:** This visually confirms that true parallelism (adding logical/physical cores) is mandatory for scaling pure computational tasks. Concurrency alone on a single core merely time-slices the execution without increasing overall throughput. On the other hand, the `Mixed` workload demonstrates steady throughput growth even on a single core, proving the efficiency of concurrency for I/O-bound tasks.

#### Plot 2: The Cost of Context Switching (Variance Spike)
* **Observation:** This bar chart focuses exclusively on the `Mixed` workload, displaying the Standard Deviation (latency variance) across different core counts. As the number of goroutines scales from 1 to 64, there is a massive spike in standard deviation (reaching over 21 ms for a single core and 10 ms for dual cores). 
* **Conclusion:** The exponential growth in variance visually represents the hidden penalty of context switching. Waking up, queueing, and managing a large pool of blocked (sleeping) goroutines introduces significant unpredictability and overhead to the Go scheduler.

#### Plot 3: Latency vs. Concurrency
* **Observation:** Evaluating the 12-core setup (`GOMAXPROCS=12`), the average latency for `CPU-bound` tasks remains exceptionally low and stable (under 3.5 ms even at 64 goroutines). However, the `Mixed` workload shows a steady, visible increase in average latency as the goroutine pool expands.
* **Conclusion:** While high concurrency in I/O-bound (Mixed) tasks dramatically improves total system throughput (as seen in Plot 1), it comes at the trade-off of higher individual task latency. This is due to the increased queueing time and scheduler multiplexing overhead as more goroutines compete for execution time.
